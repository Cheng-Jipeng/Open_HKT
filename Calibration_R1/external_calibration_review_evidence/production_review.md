# Production-parameter evidence: M10 and M12

Checked 27 September 2026 against `V6_parameter_calibration_groups.tex` (authoritative), with equation checks in `V6_production.tex`. This branch covers only the two inverse markups and two final-sector CES curvatures. It supplies candidate evidence, not an executed calibration. All numerical transformations below are our arithmetic, not estimates reported directly in V6 units by the papers.

## Exact definitions and mapping conditions

**M10, \(\vartheta_i\), \(i\in\{US,W\}\).** In the normalized variety aggregator, \(X_i=N_i^{1-1/\vartheta_i}(\int_0^{N_i}x_i(j)^{\vartheta_i}dj)^{1/\vartheta_i}\). Each intermediate uses skilled labor one-for-one, so its price is \(p_i=w_{H,i}/\vartheta_i\). Thus the gross price/marginal-cost markup is \(\mu_i=1/\vartheta_i\), and the variety demand elasticity is \(\epsilon_i=1/(1-\vartheta_i)=\mu_i/(\mu_i-1)\). A markup of 61% means \(\mu=1.61\), not 0.61. The variety elasticity is distinct from the final-sector substitution elasticity. V6 uses a common, constant within-country markup and has no production overhead in this intermediate sector. Empirical sales-weighted averages over heterogeneous firms are therefore conditional proxies; their inverse is not the sales-weighted average of firm-level inverse markups.

**M12, \(\rho_i\).** The final-good CES is \(F_i=[\alpha_i\widetilde X_i^{1-\rho_i}+(1-\alpha_i)\widetilde L_i^{1-\rho_i}]^{1/(1-\rho_i)}\), where \(\widetilde X_i=A_{X,i}X_i\), \(\widetilde L_i=A_{L,i}L_i\). Its substitution elasticity is \(\sigma_i=1/\rho_i\). Symmetry implies \(X_i=\varphi_iH_i\), not total skilled labor when R&D is positive. In particular,

\[
\log(w_{H,i}/w_{L,i})=\log\!\left(\vartheta_i\alpha_i/(1-\alpha_i)\right)+(1-\rho_i)\log(A_{X,i}/A_{L,i})-\rho_i\log(X_i/L_i).
\]

Mapping an empirical skill-supply coefficient to \(\rho_i\) requires controls for factor augmentations, the production/R&D share, skill composition, and markups. These elasticities are dimensionless; changing annual to cohort time does not exponentiate them. The implemented restriction \(\rho_{US}>1\) means \(\sigma_{US}<1\). The closest skill-substitution evidence below commonly lies on the other side of one. It must remain in the candidate set even though that requires a different computational specification.

## Evidence ledger

### P1 — Katz and Murphy (1992): estimated skill substitution

[Changes in Relative Wages, 1963–1987: Supply and Demand Factors](https://doi.org/10.2307/2118323), *QJE* 107(1), 35–78. [Author-hosted original](https://lkatz.scholars.harvard.edu/sites/g/files/omnuum5961/files/lkatz/files/changes_in_relative_wages_1963-1987_supply_and_demand_factors.pdf).

Equation (19), p. 69, estimates the log relative-wage response to relative college/high-school-equivalent labor supply as **−0.709 (SE 0.150)**, controlling a linear time trend; p. 72 reports **\(\sigma\simeq1.41\)**. The study uses annual U.S. CPS wage and labor-supply series, 1963–1987, with efficiency-unit aggregation and a competitive two-labor CES interpretation. This is an OLS estimate conditional on the technology trend, not an independently identified technology process. The V6 proxy is **\(\rho=0.709\)** (or 0.7092 from rounded 1.41). The paper also plots **0.5, 1.41, 4** as alternative elasticities on p. 72; 0.5 and 4 are scenarios, not estimated bounds. Transfer is qualified: the source uses education aggregates rather than the V6 production input \(\varphi H\), and omits V6 markups and endogenous innovation allocation.

### P2 — Ciccone and Peri (2005): instrumental-variable evidence and uncertainty

[Long-Run Substitutability Between More and Less Educated Workers: Evidence from U.S. States, 1950–1990](https://doi.org/10.1162/003465305775098233), *Review of Economics and Statistics* 87(4), 652–663. [Author-hosted published paper](https://giovanniperi.ucdavis.edu/uploads/5/6/8/2/56826033/ciccone_peri_long_run_substitutability_%40005.pdf).

Table 4, panel B(iv), column 1 (p. 656), reports **\(\sigma=1.50\), SE 0.44**, using Fuller-modified LIML (constant 4), state/time effects, and compulsory-schooling/child-labor-law instruments. IV estimates across Table 4's education aggregations and estimators span **1.20–2.00**, not a confidence interval; fixed-effects OLS instead gives **2.85–3.44**. Five decennial censuses cover 48 contiguous states (240 observations). Wage samples are U.S.-born white full-time males aged 40–50; baseline supply uses white males aged 21–59. The schooling margin and aggregation differ from KM92; weak-instrument sensitivity is explicitly investigated. The preferred proxy is **\(\rho=2/3\)**, with IV point-sensitivity **0.50–0.833**. This is long-run skill substitution, not an intermediate/labor estimate or RoW estimate. Report its sampling uncertainty separately from the chosen V6 robustness grid; do not call the grid a statistical confidence interval.

### P3 — Caselli and Coleman (2006): calibration convention, not a new elasticity estimate

[The World Technology Frontier](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.96.3.499), *AER* 96(3), 499–522. [Author-hosted published paper](https://personal.lse.ac.uk/casellif/papers/wtf.pdf).

Section I.B and Table 2 (pp. 504–505) **calibrate** the skilled/unskilled elasticity to **1.4**, borrowing KM92; robustness values are **1.1, 1.4, 1.7, 2**. In its notation the CES exponent is \(s\), with elasticity \(1/(1-s)\); hence **\(\rho_{V6}=1-s\)**, yielding **0.714** and sensitivity **0.909, 0.714, 0.588, 0.500**. This is a static 52-country development-accounting exercise with physical capital and alternative schooling thresholds, not a time-frequency discount calibration. The model backs out skill-specific efficiencies using quantities and skill premia. It is useful for conditional production calibration and cross-country heterogeneity but provides no independent evidence that \(\sigma=1.4\). Its positive CES exponent must not be copied as V6's \(\rho\).

### P4 — Krusell, Ohanian, Ríos-Rull and Violante (2000): different CES nests

[Capital-Skill Complementarity and Inequality: A Macroeconomic Analysis](https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-0262.00150), *Econometrica* 68(5), 1029–1053. [Published paper](https://www.jonathanheathcote.com/KORV.pdf).

Table II (p. 1041) reports **1.67** for unskilled labor versus equipment (and, by the specified nesting, skilled labor), but **0.67** for equipment versus skilled labor. The structural estimates use two-step simulated pseudo-maximum likelihood, annual U.S. CPS skill wages/quantities and capital/quality-adjusted equipment prices over 1963–1992. The production function distinguishes structures, equipment, and two labor types with latent labor efficiency. The outer-nest analogy suggests **\(\rho_{V6}=1/1.67=0.599\)**. Crucially, inverting the **0.67** inner-nest estimate to obtain **1.493** would map the wrong input pair and cannot validate V6's \(\rho_{US}>1\). Both coefficients are evidence about that source's nested technology, not estimates of the V6 two-input technology. Its skill-threshold sensitivity also yields 1.89 (footnote 16, p. 1044), reinforcing the importance of definitions.

### P5 — De Loecker, Eeckhout and Unger (2020): higher-markup estimates

[The Rise of Market Power and the Macroeconomic Implications](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjz041), *QJE* 135(2), 561–644. [Author manuscript, 15 November 2019](https://www.janeeckhout.com/wp-content/uploads/RMP.pdf).

Abstract and Section 3.1/Figure 1 (author manuscript pp. 9–10) report sales-weighted **\(\mu=1.21\) in 1980 and 1.61 in 2016**, implying **\(\vartheta=0.826\) and 0.621**. These are estimated period-specific aggregate markups, not a confidence range. Equation (7) identifies markup as a flexible input's output elasticity divided by its revenue share. Annual Compustat data cover U.S. listed firms, 1955–2016, with production-function estimation and sector/time-specific elasticities; Census comparisons address coverage. The median changes little while upper-tail markups and sales reallocation matter. Mapping to V6 is algebraically exact conditional on matching price/marginal cost, but economically qualified by aggregation, selected firms, overhead/variable-cost treatment, and a heterogeneous multi-sector economy. The V6 homogeneous intermediate sector cannot simultaneously inherit these markups and empirical profit shares without auditing the missing overhead and capital costs.

### P6 — Traina (2018): lower-markup alternative, retain disagreement

[Is Aggregate Market Power Increasing? Production Trends Using Financial Statements](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.3120849), working paper, February 2018. Original body retrieved through Scite, not inferred from citing articles.

Section “Longer Time Horizons,” discussion of Figure 5, reports approximately **\(\mu=1.15\) in 1950, 1.10 in 1980, and 1.15 at the sample end**, implying **\(\vartheta=0.870, 0.909, 0.870\)**. Industry-specific Cobb–Douglas production functions use annual U.S. Compustat, 1950–2016, with operating expenses (COGS plus SG&A) as variable input; a three-year production-window check gives a similar path. Utilities and financials are excluded. These are empirical estimates in a working paper, not a calibrated low-markup convention. The interpretation of marketing/management expenses as variable costs is central and disputed. Treat this as a distinct measurement specification from DLEU20, not an endpoint of the same statistical confidence band. Transfer remains qualified by firm selection, technology and cost definition; it justifies retaining low-markup sensitivity.

### P7 — De Loecker and Eeckhout (2021 version): geographic scope for RoW

[Global Market Power](https://doi.org/10.3386/w24768), NBER WP 24768, [10 February 2021 manuscript](https://crei.cat/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/GLOBAL.pdf) (first draft 2018).

Table 1 (manuscript p. 13) reports 2016 sales-weighted gross markups: **Europe 1.63, Asia 1.45, Africa 1.38, Oceania 1.55, South America 1.59**; their inverses are **0.613, 0.690, 0.725, 0.645, 0.629**. **U.S. 1.84** implies **0.543**, differing from P5's 1.61 under a different database/specification. Section 2 constructs annual markups over 1980–2016 for 67,491 firms in 134 countries (745,958 firm-years), using listed-firm financial statements and U.S.-estimated industry output elasticities. Headquarter location differs from where multinational production occurs. The paper is a geographic plausibility check: no row is “the rest of the world excluding the U.S.” for V6, and the global aggregate includes the U.S. A RoW benchmark requires a declared country/firm universe and weights; do not silently use the global 1.61 as the RoW value.

### P8/P9 — Markup identification: preserve the methodological dispute

Bond, Hashemi, Kaplan and Zoch (2021), [Some Unpleasant Markup Arithmetic](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmoneco.2021.05.004), *Journal of Monetary Economics* 121, 1–14, argue that a revenue elasticity cannot simply replace an output elasticity in the markup ratio and that flexible-input and demand-shifting assumptions matter. The abstract was retrieved through Scite; the [author-hosted published paper](https://arshiahashemi.com/publication/jme-2021-bhkz/JME-2021-BHKZ.pdf) appeared in search but full retrieval timed out. It supplies a methodological qualification, not a numerical lower bound or new calibration point.

De Loecker (2021), [Comment on (Un)pleasant ... by Bond et al (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmoneco.2021.04.009), *JME* 121, 15–18, agrees that substituting the revenue elasticity yields unity under the stated static first-order condition, while emphasizing additional prices/quantities or structural assumptions as remedies. Its introduction was read in Scite full text. Hence the debate is about identification assumptions and implementation, not proof that every production-based markup must equal one. Neither paper estimates V6's parameter.

### P10 — Oberfield and Raval (2021): complementarity on a different input margin

[Micro Data and Macro Technology](https://doi.org/10.3982/ECTA12807), *Econometrica* 89(2), 703–732. [Author-hosted published paper](https://ezraoberfield.github.io/CESAggregation.pdf).

The abstract and p. 705 give an aggregate U.S.-manufacturing capital–labor elasticity **0.5–0.7**, versus plant-level estimates **0.3–0.5**. Cross-sectional local wages, instrumented with amenities and shift-share labor-supply measures, identify plant responses; observed heterogeneity then determines reallocation and aggregation. Census of Manufactures and Annual Survey of Manufactures support aggregate paths for **1972–2007**. Figure 4b (p. 719), holding micro elasticities at their 1997 values, gives **0.60 to 0.54** over that period. This is annual/census production evidence, not a calibrated convention. Reciprocals **1.43–2.00** provide a **capital–labor analogy** for V6 \(\rho\), with **1.67** from 0.60. The source does not impose a stable aggregate CES. Its physical capital versus total labor margin differs from V6's skilled-production input versus unskilled labor, so this evidence cannot adjudicate the V6 margin merely because it supports complementarity.

### P11 — Gechert, Havranek, Irsova and Kolcunova (2022): bias-corrected meta-evidence

[Measuring Capital-Labor Substitution: The Importance of Method Choices and Publication Bias](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.red.2021.05.003), *Review of Economic Dynamics* 45, 55–82. [Author-hosted published paper](https://meta-analysis.cz/sigma/sigma2.pdf).

The review codes **3,186 estimates from 121 studies** (literature published before 2019), uses nonlinear publication-bias corrections and Bayesian/frequentist model averaging over methodological choices. Table 9 (p. 77) gives a synthetic best-practice **\(\sigma=0.30\), 95% CI [−0.01,0.60]**; the country-data variant is **0.50 [0.18,0.81]**, and quarterly-data variant **0.42 [0.08,0.76]**. These are conditional meta-estimates, across populations, frequencies and production environments, not a universal structural coefficient. The point inversions are **3.33, 2.00, 2.38**. **Do not invert the baseline CI crossing zero into a bounded positive V6 interval.** This is evidence for complementary physical capital and labor under stated methodological preferences, not an estimate of V6's \(\varphi H\)-versus-\(L\) margin. It supports a separate broad complementarity scenario branch while preserving skill-substitution evidence; averaging the two margins would be economically misleading.

### P12 — Havranek, Irsova, Laslopova and Zeynalova (2024): revised skill meta-evidence

[Publication and Attenuation Biases in Measuring Skill Substitution](https://doi.org/10.1162/rest_a_01227), *Review of Economics and Statistics* **106(5), 1187–1200 (2024 issue; online 2022)**. [Final published PDF](https://meta-analysis.cz/skill/skill2.pdf).

This analysis collects 682 estimates from 77 studies and separates direct from inverse regressions. Nonlinear publication-bias correction, IV/endogeneity comparisons, and model averaging yield substantially greater skill substitution. Table 5 (p. 1197), subjective-best-practice column, reports negative inverse elasticities: **all countries −0.27, 95% credible interval [−0.48,−0.05] (\(\sigma=3.7\)); U.S. −0.16 [−0.38,0.06] (\(\sigma=6.3\)); developing countries −0.47 [−0.70,−0.24] (\(\sigma=2.1\))**. Under the conditional skill mapping, V6 candidates are **\(\rho=0.27,0.16,0.47\)**. The U.S. interval maps to **[−0.06,0.38]**, crosses zero, and must not be represented as a finite positive elasticity interval. Data frequencies/environments vary; subjective best practice favors annual disaggregated panel data, multilevel CES, fixed effects, and valid IV. This is a meta-estimate, not a fresh V6 estimate; its IV-validity and bias-correction assumptions remain consequential. The 2020/2021 working-paper version entitled “Skilled and Unskilled Labor Are Less Substitutable than Commonly Thought,” reporting 0.6–0.9, is **superseded**, not independent contradictory evidence.

**Update to the CES candidate table following P12.** Retain the original-study skill anchor \(\rho=0.667\), but give equal visibility to the newer bias-corrected alternatives **0.27 (all-country mean) and 0.16 (U.S.)**; do not describe 1.5 as a consensus elasticity. Expand the **analyst skill-proxy scenario envelope to 0.16–0.91**, with a **0.05** high-substitution stress point suggested by the lower endpoint of P12's all-country inverse-elasticity credible interval. For RoW, P12's **0.47 developing-country** candidate is another conditional composition-dependent anchor. This envelope is not P12's credible interval and does not resolve the factor-input mapping. The separate **capital–labor-analogy branch 1.43–3.33** remains distinct. Neither the traditional nor the newer skill-proxy central estimates run under the current U.S. \(\rho>1\) restriction.

For both CES parameters, recommended range endpoints represent specification sensitivity; source posterior credible intervals are labeled separately. For both markup parameters, intervals combine competing measurement definitions and dates, and must not be portrayed as sampling uncertainty. No values were selected because they satisfy a V6 theorem.
